01

Introduction

Cloud computing has changed how organisations build, deploy and manage applications. Instead of relying only on physical infrastructure, organisations can use cloud services based on their requirements and pay for the resources they need. During this module, I explored the main cloud service models, including Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), Platform as a Service (PaaS) and Software as a Service (SaaS), together with public, private and hybrid deployment approaches (Mell and Grance, 2011).

The module also reinforced that cloud computing is not only about moving applications to the cloud. Architecture decisions involve security, scalability, cost, resilience and operational responsibility (Gupta, 2024). This e-Portfolio reflects on five areas selected across the module: cloud fundamentals from Unit 1, hybrid and edge computing from Unit 6, disaster recovery from Unit 8, serverless computing from Unit 10, and AI in cloud computing from Unit 11. Units 10–12 also introduced emerging directions in cloud computing, particularly serverless and AI-enabled cloud services, which inform my future learning goals.

02

Weekly Reflections

2.1 Week 1 / Unit 1: Cloud Fundamentals and Service Models

I was already familiar with IaaS, PaaS and SaaS before this module, but Unit 1 helped me think about them more from an architectural and business point of view. Mell and Grance (2011) describe the service models in terms of what is provided by the cloud provider and what remains under the customer’s control. This made the relationship between flexibility and operational responsibility clearer to me.

What became more obvious is that more control is not always better. A virtual machine provides flexibility but also requires responsibility for operating system maintenance, patching and configuration, while a managed PaaS service removes some of that work. This was reflected in the Azure infrastructure exercise, where Azure App Service and Azure SQL Database provided a simpler managed approach than building the same solution around virtual machines (see Appendix A).

The main lesson for me was that the service model should be selected according to the workload and organisational requirements rather than simply choosing the option that provides the most technical control.

2.2 Week 6 / Unit 6: Hybrid, Fog and Edge Computing

The discussion on hybrid, fog and edge computing helped me think more carefully about where workloads and data should be processed. I previously viewed cloud computing mainly around centralised cloud infrastructure, but some applications need faster responses or generate enough data that sending everything to a distant cloud service is inefficient.

Gupta et al. (2017) explain that fog computing extends cloud services towards the edge of the network and can reduce latency and network congestion. Zheng et al. (2024) demonstrate a similar idea in vehicular networks, where frequently requested data is cached on nearby edge nodes to reduce retrieval latency. Their design also stores popular data across two nodes, which helps reduce the risk of a single point of failure.

What I found important is that edge computing does not replace the cloud. Both can work together. Time-sensitive processing can happen closer to users or devices, while the cloud can provide larger-scale storage and processing. However, distributing workloads also increases complexity around networking, security, monitoring and data consistency. This made me see edge and hybrid architecture as a trade-off between performance and operational complexity rather than simply a way of making systems faster.

2.3 Week 8 / Unit 8: Disaster Recovery and Business Continuity

The topic of disaster recovery and business continuity made me think beyond simply keeping a system highly available. A system may tolerate individual component failures and still require a recovery plan for larger incidents such as data corruption, regional outages or major infrastructure failure.

Gupta (2024) discusses backups, replication, failover and recovery testing as important parts of disaster recovery planning. What stood out to me was the importance of testing the recovery process rather than assuming that backups or redundant infrastructure will work when needed. Having a backup is different from knowing that it can actually be restored.

This became more practical when I designed a disaster recovery plan during the module. The exercise required me to consider backup, failover and recovery requirements instead of treating availability and recoverability as the same thing. However, because I did not perform and record a live recovery or failover test, I view this as a DR design exercise rather than a validated recovery implementation.

It showed me that recovery objectives should influence architecture before deployment and that a recovery strategy should eventually be tested against measurable RTO and RPO targets.

2.4 Week 10 / Unit 10: Serverless Computing

I was already familiar with serverless computing before this module, particularly the idea of running code without managing the underlying servers. The module helped me look at it more critically and consider when it is appropriate rather than focusing only on convenience.

Kumar and Mahendrakar (2019) describe benefits such as event-driven execution, consumption-based pricing and automatic scaling. These characteristics are useful where workloads are unpredictable or functions only need to run when an event occurs. Toosi et al. (2025) also identify scalability, reduced infrastructure management and faster application development as benefits of the serverless model.

The limitations are equally important. Toosi et al. (2025) highlight challenges such as cold starts, state management, debugging, resource management, security and compliance. Ni et al. (2024) focus specifically on security and show that serverless risks can exist across the cloud, container infrastructure, serverless and access layers. Kumar and Mahendrakar (2019) also discuss vendor-specific resources and cross-platform monitoring as disadvantages.

The main lesson for me was that serverless reduces some operational responsibilities, but it does not remove architectural responsibility. Security, monitoring, performance, cost and provider dependency still need to be considered before deciding that serverless is the right approach.

2.5 Week 11 / Unit 11: AI and Cloud Computing

The topic of AI and cloud computing showed how cloud platforms can make AI services easier to adopt by providing scalable computing, storage and managed services. Gupta (2024) discusses the use of AI and machine learning in areas such as automation, anomaly detection, predictive analysis and data-driven decision-making.

What I found useful was seeing AI as part of a wider cloud architecture rather than as a separate feature. An AI capability still depends on data, storage, security, monitoring and suitable compute resources. Cloud platforms can reduce the effort required to build some of these components from scratch.

However, managed AI services should not automatically be added to a solution simply because they are available. The quality of the output depends heavily on the data, while privacy, cost, governance and provider dependency also need to be considered. For me, AI should solve a clear business problem and provide enough value to justify the additional complexity.

03

Skills Development

The practical activities allowed me to apply cloud concepts rather than only discuss them theoretically. Although I already had professional cloud experience, the exercises helped me evaluate architecture choices more systematically.

One area I developed further was Infrastructure as Code. During the module, I worked with Azure Resource Manager (ARM) and later used Terraform when developing the BenLogistics solution. The Azure exercise showed how resources such as App Service, Azure SQL Database and networking components can be defined as code rather than created manually (see Appendix A). My Terraform work allowed me to apply the same principle to an AWS-based architecture. The configuration was initialised, validated and planned successfully, providing evidence that the Infrastructure as Code definition was syntactically valid and that Terraform could resolve the proposed AWS resources (see Appendix B). I did not run terraform apply, so I treat this as configuration and pre-deployment validation rather than evidence of a live deployment.

Using both made me compare the frameworks rather than treating Infrastructure as Code as one approach. ARM was suitable for the Azure exercise because it integrates directly with Azure resources, while Terraform provided the advantage of supporting multiple cloud providers. However, using Terraform does not automatically make an architecture portable. If an application depends heavily on provider-specific services, moving it can still require significant redesign. Opara-Martins, Sahandi and Tian (2016) identify interoperability and portability as important factors in cloud vendor lock-in.

CloudFormation is another option for BenLogistics because it integrates closely with AWS, but this also makes it provider-specific. Regvart, Vlahović and Balković (2026) compare Terraform, Pulumi and AWS CloudFormation and identify differences in deployment performance and maintainability. This reinforced that the IaC tool should be selected according to the environment and requirements rather than assuming one tool is always better.

The disaster recovery design exercise also developed my practical understanding of resilience by requiring me to consider failure scenarios, backups, failover and recovery objectives rather than only normal system operation. Overall, the module strengthened how I compare cloud options based on cost, portability, security, resilience and operational effort.

04

Application to Industry

The logistics industry shows where cloud computing can provide practical value because logistics systems deal with changing demand, real-time information and large amounts of operational data. During the module, I applied these ideas to BenLogistics, a hypothetical logistics company that needed improved scalability, delivery visibility, automation and cost control. The proposed architecture is included in Appendix C.

A cloud-based solution can support services such as shipping labels, delivery tracking and delivery scheduling. Scalability is particularly relevant because demand can change significantly during seasonal peaks. Instead of maintaining enough infrastructure for the highest expected demand throughout the year, cloud resources can scale according to workload.

However, I do not think every existing logistics system should automatically be moved to the cloud. A company may already depend on transport management or warehouse management systems that are expensive or risky to replace immediately. A hybrid approach can allow those systems to remain on-premises while newer customer-facing services and data capabilities are introduced in the cloud. The trade-off is additional integration, networking, security and monitoring requirements.

AI also has practical applications in logistics. Nowacki and Wierzbic (2024) examine AI for transport demand planning in a logistics organisation, showing how operational data can support planning decisions. This is relevant to BenLogistics because historical shipment and demand data could be used for forecasting rather than relying only on static planning.

Edge computing may provide value where decisions depend on current road or vehicle information. Peng et al. (2024) demonstrate a cloud-edge approach in which edge devices monitor changing road conditions and the cloud supports dynamic delivery route adjustment. This shows a practical use of edge computing in logistics, although the latency benefit must still be balanced against the added complexity of distributed infrastructure.

The main point I took from applying the module to logistics is that cloud adoption should start with the business problem. Scalability, managed services, serverless technologies, AI and edge computing can provide value, but using more cloud services does not automatically produce a better architecture. The benefits need to be balanced against migration risk, security, cost, resilience and operational complexity.

05

Future Learning Goals

My future development will focus on three measurable areas.

First, by January 2027, I plan to develop a reusable Terraform module for a small cloud environment and integrate terraform fmt, validate and plan checks into a CI/CD pipeline. I will consider this successful when the module can be reused across at least two environments with the validation pipeline completing successfully.

Second, by March 2027, I plan to build and test a small multi-region disaster recovery prototype. I will define an RTO of less than 30 minutes and an RPO of less than 5 minutes, perform a documented recovery or failover test, and compare the observed recovery results with these targets. This builds directly on the disaster recovery design work completed during Unit 8.

Finally, by June 2027, I plan to build small serverless, managed PaaS and edge-oriented prototypes and compare them using performance, cost, security and operational complexity. Serverless computing, discussed in Unit 10, still presents challenges around cold starts, state, debugging and security (Toosi et al., 2025), while the later units also demonstrated the growing importance of AI-enabled cloud services. My aim is to document when each architectural approach provides sufficient benefit to justify its additional complexity.

These goals will allow me to move from understanding cloud technologies individually towards evaluating them using measurable architectural and operational requirements.

06

Conclusion

Overall, this module strengthened how I approach cloud architecture. It encouraged me to move beyond individual services and consider the trade-offs created by each architectural decision.

One of the most important lessons for me is that reducing operational responsibility often shifts responsibility rather than removing it. PaaS and serverless reduce infrastructure management but can increase provider dependency. Hybrid and edge architectures can improve control or responsiveness but introduce additional operational complexity. Infrastructure as Code improves consistency, but using a portable tool does not automatically make the architecture portable.

Going forward, I would start with workload and business requirements, identify the required control, resilience and performance, and choose the simplest architecture that meets them. This is the main change in how I now evaluate cloud solutions.
